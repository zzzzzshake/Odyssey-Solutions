Holiday Scams That Target Small Offices: A Checklist for Your Staff

The fake bank alerts, toll texts, gift card requests, code requests and overpaid checks that hit offices in December, and the one safe move for each.

Published November 23, 20265-minute readPublished by Odyssey Solutions

The weeks between Thanksgiving and New Year's are busy for offices and busy for scammers. Staff are rushing, people are out, packages are arriving and everyone is buying something. Most holiday scams work because they ask for one fast action at the wrong moment.

This is a one-page checklist you can share with your team. Each scam gets one sign to watch for and one safe move.

The one rule behind all of them

If a message you didn't expect asks you to tap, pay, reply or read back a code, stop. Then check it a different way: a number you already have, an app you already use, or a website you type in yourself. Never use the phone number or link inside the message.

1. The fake bank fraud alert

What it looks like: "Did you make a $980 purchase? Reply YES or NO." A reply leads to a call from a "fraud department" that offers to help and then moves money out of the account.

Why it works: It looks like protection. Fake fraud alerts were one of the FTC's top five text scams of 2024, a year when people reported $470 million in losses to text scams overall. FTC Data Spotlight: Top text scams of 2024

Safe move: Don't reply. Call the number on the back of the card or on your statement. Your bank will never ask you to move money to "protect" it. FTC: Never move your money to protect it

2. The unpaid toll text

What it looks like: "Unpaid toll balance due today. Pay now to avoid suspension," with a link.

Safe move: Don't tap or reply. Check your account on your toll agency's real website or app, typed in yourself. Forward the text to 7726 and delete it. FTC: Got a text about unpaid tolls? It's probably a scam

3. The package that couldn't be delivered

What it looks like: A delivery text with a link to "update your address."

Safe move: Check tracking from your order email on the carrier's real site. The Postal Service only texts you if you asked for tracking, and those texts don't have links. Our package text scam guide has the details. USPIS: Package tracking text scams

4. "I sent you a code by mistake"

What it looks like: A verification code arrives, then someone texts or calls asking you to read it back. Sometimes they say they're your bank.

Safe move: Never share a verification code with anyone who contacted you. The FTC says anyone who asks for your code is a scammer, including someone who says they're from your bank's fraud department. FTC: What's a verification code and why would someone ask me for it?

5. The boss who wants gift cards

What it looks like: An email or text that looks like it's from the owner or doctor: "Quick favor. Buy five gift cards and send me the codes."

Safe move: Don't reply to that message. Call your boss on a number you already know. Any request to pay with gift card codes is a red flag. FTC: No, that's not your boss asking you to buy gift cards

6. The customer who overpaid

What it looks like: A check for more than the invoice, then a polite request to send back the difference by payment app or wire.

Safe move: Never send money back to someone who says they overpaid you. The check is fake and bounces later, and you owe the bank. Funds showing as available doesn't mean the check is good. FTC: Fake check scam targets childcare providers

7. The QR code sticker

What it looks like: A QR code on a parking meter or sign that's really a sticker over the real one.

Safe move: Look for a sticker on top. Read the web address in your camera's preview before opening it, or pay at the meter or in an app you already use. FTC: See a QR code parked somewhere? Don't scan it yet

8. The deal that's too good

What it looks like: A store you've never heard of with 70% off, or a familiar brand at an address with "outlet" or "sale" added.

Safe move: Search the store name with "scam" or "reviews" before buying, and pay with a credit card so you can dispute the charge. FTC: How to avoid an online shopping scam this holiday season

If someone already fell for one

Speed matters more than blame.

  1. Call the bank or card company right away, using the number on the card.
  2. Change any password that was typed into a fake page, and turn on two-step sign-in.
  3. Tell whoever handles your office IT the same day, especially if a work email or work device was involved.
  4. Report it at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.

Print this for the break room

  • We don't tap links in texts we didn't expect.
  • We don't read codes to anyone.
  • We don't buy gift cards because an email said so.
  • We don't send back "overpayments."
  • When in doubt, we call a number we already have.

FAQ

What is the most reported text scam?

According to the FTC, fake package delivery texts were the most reported text scam in 2024. Fake fraud alerts and unpaid toll texts were also in the top five.

How do I report a scam text?

Forward it to 7726 (SPAM), then delete it. You can also report it at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.

Should I warn my staff about these scams?

Yes. Most of these scams target whoever picks up the phone or opens the email first. A short checklist in the break room and a reminder in your team meeting go a long way.
